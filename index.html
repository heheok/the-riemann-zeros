What's going on here?

There's a famous function in maths called the zeta function. You give it a number and it hands you a number back. For a few very special inputs it hands back exactly zero. Those inputs are called the zeros, and mathematicians have been obsessed with them since 1859.

This page finds those zeros one at a time and plays a note for each one. Nobody wrote the melody. It comes from where the zeros are.

The swirly thing

The big curve is the function's answer, drawn as a moving dot. It loops all over the place, but it keeps coming back through the exact middle. Every time it crosses the middle, that's a zero, and that's when you hear a note.

How a zero turns into a note

The zeros aren't evenly spaced. Sometimes the next one shows up quickly, sometimes you have to wait. If the wait is longer than usual, the tune steps up and the note gets held. If it's shorter than usual, the tune steps down and the note is quick. So the melody is really the spacing of the zeros, turned into sound.

Where the drops come from

Between two zeros the curve wanders off and comes back. Usually that's a small loop. Once in a while it swings way out. When that happens you'll see "Big loop" appear, and a bit later the kick drum cuts out, the tension builds for four bars, and the drop hits. We didn't pick when that happens. The function did.

Each drop also plays the tune a little differently, so no two sound the same. The first is plain. Then it bounces between octaves, then picks up a harmony, then stutters, then flips upside down, and round again.

The numbers in the box

That's the actual maths, live: which zero is playing, how long the wait for the next one is compared to normal, and how big the current loop is. You don't need to follow the formulas to enjoy the track. They're there so you can see nothing is faked.

Why does anyone care about these zeros?

Because they secretly run the prime numbers. Primes (2, 3, 5, 7, 11 and so on) look like they pop up at random, but the positions of these zeros decide exactly where they land. The Riemann hypothesis is a guess from 1859 that every single zero sits on one straight line. People spent 167 years trying to prove it. This page walks up that line and plays whatever it finds, and since there are infinitely many zeros, it never has to stop.

The small print: the zeros and their spacing are real maths, worked out in your browser while you listen. The drums, the scale, the tempo and the rule for turning a wait into a note are choices we made so it would sound good. Someone else could take the same zeros and make a completely different track. This page doesn't prove anything, and it has nothing to do with anyone's proof. Made in a conversation with Claude.
